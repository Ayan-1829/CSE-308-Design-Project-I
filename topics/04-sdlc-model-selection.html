SDLC Model Selection

The six stages of the software development life cycle, the waterfall, iterative, V, spiral, agile and prototyping models, and how to choose one with a weighted comparison matrix.

Before we start

Why this matters

Two teams build the same library system. One plans everything up front and tests at the end; the other shows the librarian a working screen every two weeks. Which one is right?

It depends on the project: how clear the requirements are, how risky it is, and how often the customer can give feedback. Choosing the process is a design decision in its own right.

In this topic you learn the main SDLC models and a systematic way to pick one — and to defend the choice in your report.

Concept 1 of 13

What the SDLC is

The Software Development Life Cycle is the process a software organisation follows to plan, build, test, deliver and maintain software. Its aim: high-quality software that meets or exceeds customer expectations, on time and within cost.

An SDLC model decides how these stages are arranged: once each in a line, repeated in loops, or in short overlapping cycles.

The SDLC and its stages

The six stages

1. Planning & requirement analysisSenior team members gather input from the customer, sales, market surveys and domain experts; plan quality assurance; identify risks; study feasibility.Feasibility report, project plan

Concept 3 of 13

Waterfall model

Phases run one after another; the next starts only when the previous one is signed off. Phases do not overlap. Described by Winston Royce (1970), who actually warned against using it without feedback.

Strengths

  • simple to understand and manage
  • clear milestones and documents
  • good when requirements are fixed

Weaknesses

  • no working software until late
  • costly to change requirements
  • high risk for long or unclear projects

Use it when the requirements are very well known, clear and fixed, and the technology is familiar.

The models

Iterative (incremental) model

Start with a simple implementation of part of the requirements, then enhance it in repeated cycles until the full system is built. Each iteration may change the design and adds new functions.

Strengths

  • working software early
  • easier to test small increments
  • changes can enter later iterations

Weaknesses

  • needs a good overall architecture up front
  • more management effort
  • total cost can grow

Use it when the main requirements are clear, the system is large, and changes are expected in future.

The models

V-model

An extension of waterfall where the test for each phase is planned at the same time as that phase: verification on the left arm, validation on the right, joined by coding at the bottom.

Strengths

  • testing is planned from day one
  • defects found early
  • strong for safety- and money-critical systems

Weaknesses

  • rigid, like waterfall
  • no early prototype
  • changes are expensive

Use it when requirements are clear, simple and stable and high reliability is essential (banking, medical).

The models

Spiral model

A project passes repeatedly through four quadrants — determine objectives, identify and resolve risks, develop and test, plan the next iteration. Each loop usually produces a prototype.

Strengths

  • explicit risk analysis every loop
  • suits large, expensive, risky projects
  • users see prototypes

Weaknesses

  • complex to manage
  • needs risk-analysis expertise
  • too heavy for small projects

Use it when the project is large, risk and cost evaluation are important and frequent releases or prototypes help.

The models

Agile model

“Agile” means swift and versatile. Work is broken into small iterations (sprints) of 1–4 weeks with constant customer feedback, following the Agile Manifesto (2001): individuals and interactions, working software, customer collaboration and responding to change.

Scrum, the most used agile framework, has a Product Owner, a Scrum Master and developers; a product backlog; and events: sprint planning, daily scrum, sprint review, retrospective.

Strengths

  • adapts to changing requirements
  • frequent working releases
  • close customer contact

Weaknesses

  • needs an available customer
  • less documentation
  • hard to fix cost and scope up front

The models

Prototyping model

A working model of the system is built quickly and shown to users to discover what they really want. The prototype is refined until approved, then the real product is engineered.

Strengths

  • users see and try the system early
  • uncovers missing requirements
  • reduces misunderstanding

Weaknesses

  • users may mistake the prototype for the product
  • can encourage endless changes
  • prototype code is often thrown away

Use it when requirements are unclear, especially for the user interface.

Choosing a model

The weighted comparison matrix

  1. Choose criteria that matter for your project (well-known requirements, risk, user testing, security…).
  2. Give each a priority (weight). The manual’s weights add up to 30.
  3. For each model, mark Yes if it supports the criterion.
  4. A model scores the sum of the weights where it says Yes.
  5. Choose the highest score — and justify it in words.
Phrase every criterion so that “Yes” is good. The manual’s “Time consuming” row gives points for Yes, which reads as a reward for being slow; rename it “Fits a fixed schedule” so the meaning is clear.

Choosing a model

Justifying the choice in your report

A good justification has four parts:

  1. The matrix with criteria, weights and totals.
  2. The winning model and its score.
  3. Why the top-weighted criteria matter for this system.
  4. How you will adapt the model (e.g. “V-model, with a UI prototype in week 4”).

Example paragraph

“We selected the V-model (22 of 30 points). Our library system’s requirements were fixed after two interviews with the librarian, and fine calculation and member data must be reliable and secure — the two criteria we weighted most. The V-model plans a test for every design phase, which suits our testing phase in weeks 7–9. Spiral tied at 22, but its risk-analysis loops are heavier than an 11-week student project needs.”

Worked example 1 of 3

Worked example 1

A hospital wants software that calculates medicine doses. Requirements come from fixed medical guidelines, and every function must be proved correct. Which model, and why?
Show the solution
V-model. Requirements are fixed and well known, and safety demands that each design level has a planned test (unit, integration, system, acceptance). Agile’s light documentation would make certification harder.

Worked example 2 of 3

Worked example 2

A start-up is building a food-delivery app. It is not sure what features customers want and competitors change every month. Which model?
Show the solution
Agile (for example Scrum). Requirements change often and the team needs working releases every few weeks to get customer feedback. Prototyping could be used inside the first sprints for the UI.

Worked example 3 of 3

Worked example 3

Criteria and weights: clear requirements (5), user feedback (4), risk handling (3). Waterfall: Yes, No, No. Prototype: No, Yes, No. Spiral: No, Yes, Yes. Compute the scores.
Show the solution
Waterfall = 5. Prototype = 4. Spiral = 4 + 3 = 7, the highest. Spiral wins with these weights; if clear requirements were weighted 8, waterfall would score 8 and win — so the choice depends on honest weights.

Practice

Practice (1 of 2)

Try each one on paper first, then check your answer.

1. Name the six SDLC stages in order.
Show the answer
Planning and requirement analysis; defining requirements (SRS); designing the architecture (DDS); building; testing; deployment and maintenance.
2. What document is produced in stage 2, and in stage 3?
Show the answer
Stage 2: the SRS (Software Requirements Specification). Stage 3: the DDS (Design Document Specification).
3. In the V-model, which test phase is paired with the requirements phase?
Show the answer
Acceptance testing: it checks the finished system against the user requirements.

4. Lab task hint: list five criteria you could use in a comparison matrix for a Library Management System.
Show the answer
For example: requirements are well known; users can test early versions; the system handles personal data securely; the team has used the technology before; delivery within 11 weeks. Your weights and ticks must be your own.
5. What are the four quadrants of the spiral model?
Show the answer
Determine objectives, alternatives and constraints; identify and resolve risks; develop and test; plan the next iteration.
6. Give one difference between the iterative model and the prototyping model.
Show the answer
Iterative builds real, usable increments of the final system; prototyping builds a quick model mainly to discover requirements, which is often thrown away before the real product is engineered.

Summary

Summary

  • The SDLC has six stages: planning, requirements (SRS), design (DDS), building, testing, deployment and maintenance.
  • Waterfall and V-model suit fixed requirements; the V-model plans a test for every phase.
  • Iterative delivers growing versions; spiral adds a risk-analysis loop for large risky projects.
  • Agile uses short sprints and constant feedback; prototyping discovers unclear requirements.
  • Choose with a weighted matrix: criteria phrased so Yes is good, honest weights, recomputed totals.
  • Justify the choice in words and say how you will adapt the model.

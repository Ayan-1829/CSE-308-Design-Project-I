Before we start

Why this matters

Most student projects do not fail because of code. They fail in week 9, when nobody knows who was writing the SRS, the diagrams contradict each other and the deadline is Thursday.

This first lab sets up everything that prevents that: a team with clear roles, a project worth doing, and a plan that says what happens each week, what it costs and what could go wrong.

You will also write the first chapter of your report: the proposal.

The big picture

What Design Project I is about

In this course your team takes one software system from an idea to a complete design. You do not just draw diagrams in isolation: every lab adds one chapter to the same project report.

Team & proposal→LaTeX report→SRS→SDLC model→DFD→Use cases→Sequence & communication→Class diagram→UI wireframes
  • Running example: the ATM banking system used throughout the lab manual.
  • Lab tasks: a Library Management System (and a Student Registration System for class diagrams).
  • Lab exercise: the same step applied to your team’s own system, submitted as a report.
Why a design course? Fixing a requirement mistake after the system is built can cost many times more than fixing it on paper. Design is where you find mistakes cheaply.

A real project, summarised

Team Pulse designs CampusCare, an appointment system for the university medical centre:

  1. Proposal: 40-minute queues → book in under 2 minutes; 11-week plan; 80,300 Tk budget.
  2. LaTeX: one Overleaf report, a chapter per member.
  3. SRS: 4 user classes, numbered FRs, 6 measurable NFRs.
  4. SDLC: iterative, 22 of 30 points.
  5. DFD: Level 0 with 4 entities; Level 1 with 5 processes, 3 stores.
  6. Use cases: 8 goals; «include» Log in, «extend» Join waiting list.
  7. Sequence & communication: Book appointment with an alt fragment.
  8. Class diagram: abstract User; Schedule ◆ Slot.
  9. Wireframes: 3 Figma screens, tested with 5 users.

Concept 2 of 20

Forming a team

A good team is small enough to coordinate and varied enough to cover every skill the project needs. Most design-project teams have 3–5 members.

  • Mix skills: someone who writes well, someone who draws diagrams, someone who codes, someone organised.
  • Agree on availability before agreeing on friendship.
  • Choose a leader (project manager) who tracks deadlines — not one who does all the work.
  • Write a team charter in the first week.

How teams develop: Tuckman’s stages

Psychologist Bruce Tuckman (1965) observed that most groups pass through the same stages. Knowing them helps you recognise that early friction is normal.

Concept 5 of 20

Choosing the project

A good project…

  • solves a real problem for real users you can talk to
  • fits the time: about 10–12 weeks of part-time work
  • has at least two kinds of user (e.g. customer and admin) — richer diagrams
  • stores and processes data — needed for DFD and class diagrams
  • is your team’s own idea, not copied from seniors

Warning signs

  • “A system like Facebook” — far too big
  • “A calculator” — too small: one user, no data
  • No one on the team understands the domain
  • Needs hardware or data you cannot get
  • Identical to last year’s submitted project
Examples that work well: clinic appointment booking, hall/dormitory meal management, lost-and-found for campus, parking slot booking, blood-donor finder, club event registration, garment-factory attendance.

The project proposal

Problem analysis and motivation

Start with the problem, not the solution. The lab manual’s ATM example says: customers of a bank must perform transactions (cash withdrawal, balance enquiry) without a bank clerk, at any time, in public places — and huge amounts of data must be kept efficient, error-free, automated and maintainable.

A useful template for your own problem statement:

[Users] need a way to [goal] because [evidence].
Today they [current workaround], which causes [cost or pain].
Write a problem statement for a campus lost-and-found system.
Show the answer
“Students and staff need a way to report and recover lost items because the notice board receives about 30 items a month and most are never claimed. Today people post on Facebook groups, which is unsearchable and loses posts within a day.”
Evidence beats adjectives. “Many students struggle” is weak; “in a survey of 40 students, 29 had lost an item this semester” is strong.

The project proposal

Writing objectives that can be checked

Objectives state what the finished system will achieve. Make them SMART: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound.

Weak objectiveSMART objective
Make banking easy.Let a customer withdraw cash in under 60 seconds from card insertion.
Build a good library system.Let a member search the catalogue by title, author or ISBN and reserve an available book online by week 10.
Improve security.Lock a card after three wrong PIN attempts and log every failed attempt.
Each objective should later map to requirements in the SRS and to a test. If you cannot test it, rewrite it.

The project proposal

Literature review: learning from what exists

A literature review shows you know the field. For a design project it usually has two parts:

  1. Background facts with sources. The manual cites ATMIA estimates of about 3 million ATMs worldwide — roughly one per 3,000 people — with high density in the USA, Canada, Europe and Japan and growing demand in Asia-Pacific and Latin America.
  2. Existing systems compared feature by feature, ending with the gap your project fills.

The project proposal

Methodology: how you will build it

The methodology describes the development approach (an SDLC model — topic 4), the tools, and the main behaviour of the system. The manual recommends prototyping: users see inputs and outputs early and changes happen before the final system is built, which reduces risk because late fixes are expensive.

  • Model: prototype / iterative / agile …
  • Tools: Overleaf, draw.io, Figma, a language and database
  • A block or state diagram of the main flow

Concept 11 of 20

Feasibility study

A feasibility study compares the proposed system with the current one and asks: should we build this at all? A common checklist is TELOS.

Technical

Do we have the technology and skills? Expected accuracy, response time, growth potential, user-friendliness.

Economic

Do benefits exceed costs? Budget, human resources, training, payback period, ROI.

Legal

Any laws, licences, data-protection or banking rules?

Operational

Will people actually use it? Operation time, reliability, accuracy, retrieval rate.

Schedule

Can it be finished by the deadline?

Planning and scheduling

Gantt charts

  • A Gantt chart (Henry Gantt, 1910s) lists tasks down the side and time across the top; each task is a bar.
  • Overlapping bars = tasks running in parallel (testing starts while coding continues).
  • A milestone is a zero-length event (proposal submitted), often drawn as a ◆.
  • A dependency means one task cannot start until another finishes (design before coding).
  • The critical path is the longest chain of dependent tasks; any delay on it delays the whole project.
Tip: plan in weeks, leave the last week as a buffer, and update the chart when reality changes — an old Gantt chart is worse than none.

Planning and scheduling

Budget planning

A budget lists every cost by category: office costs (meetings, first aid), website, equipment (computers, ATM machines, cameras) and salaries. The manual compares the existing system with the new system to show savings.

  • Group costs into categories with subtotals.
  • Show one-time costs (equipment) separately from recurring costs (salaries, maintenance) when you can.
  • State the currency and period (Tk per project, per month…).
  • Add a contingency line (often 5–10%) for surprises.
Checked: the manual’s existing total of 21,93,000 Tk and new total of 20,69,000 Tk are the correct sums of its rows — a saving of 1,24,000 Tk (5.7%).

Concept 18 of 20

Risk analysis

  1. Identify risks: people, technology, requirements, schedule, cost.
  2. Analyse: estimate probability and impact (1–5 each); score = P × I.
  3. Plan a response: avoid (change the plan), reduce (lower P or I), transfer (insurance, vendor) or accept.
  4. Monitor weekly; risks change.
RiskResponse
A member leavesReduce: pair people on each deliverable
Laptop diesReduce: work in Overleaf / Git, weekly backup
Scope creepAvoid: freeze the SRS; log change requests
Tool unfamiliarReduce: 1-hour tutorial in week 1

Worked example 1 of 3

Worked example 1

A team of four has 11 weeks. Coding needs design to be finished; testing needs at least two weeks of coding. Design takes 3 weeks and starts in week 2. When can testing start at the earliest, and what is on the critical path?
Show the solution
  1. Design: weeks 2–4.
  2. Coding can start in week 5.
  3. Testing needs two weeks of coding (weeks 5–6), so it can start in week 7.
  4. Critical path: planning → design → coding → testing → delivery. A delay in design pushes everything after it.

Worked example 2 of 3

Worked example 2

Your system costs 6,00,000 Tk to build and 60,000 Tk a year to run. It saves 2,40,000 Tk a year in staff time. Find the payback period and the 3-year ROI.
Show the solution
  1. Net benefit per year = 2,40,000 − 60,000 = 1,80,000 Tk.
  2. Payback = 6,00,000 ÷ 1,80,000 = 3.3 years.
  3. 3-year cost = 6,00,000 + 3 × 60,000 = 7,80,000; benefit = 3 × 2,40,000 = 7,20,000.
  4. ROI = (7,20,000 − 7,80,000) ÷ 7,80,000 = −7.7%: not yet profitable after 3 years; it pays back early in year 4.

Worked example 3 of 3

Worked example 3

Rewrite as a SMART objective: “The system will make the library better for students.”
Show the solution
“By the end of week 10, the system will let a registered student search the catalogue by title, author or ISBN and see within 2 seconds whether a copy is available.” It is specific (search and availability), measurable (2 seconds), achievable, relevant to students and time-bound (week 10).

Practice

Practice (1 of 2)

Try each one on paper first, then check your answer.

1. Name the five TELOS feasibility types and give one question for each for a library management system.
Show the answer
Technical: can we build barcode scanning with our skills? Economic: do the savings in librarian time justify the cost? Legal: is storing student data allowed under university policy? Operational: will librarians actually switch from registers? Schedule: can it be finished in 11 weeks?
2. What is the difference between a milestone and a task on a Gantt chart?
Show the answer
A task has a duration and is drawn as a bar; a milestone is a point in time with zero duration (e.g. “SRS approved”), usually drawn as a diamond.
3. A risk has probability 4 and impact 2; another has probability 2 and impact 5. Which is scored higher, and why might you still treat the second one seriously?
Show the answer
Scores are 8 and 10, so the second is higher. Even with a lower score, a high-impact risk (5) such as losing all work deserves a cheap reduction step (backups), because its consequences cannot be recovered.

4. Lab task hint: list the budget categories you would use for a Library Management System (do not fill in numbers here).
Show the answer
For example: equipment (computers, barcode scanners, printer), software (licences, hosting, domain), salaries (developer, designer, trainer), training for librarians, maintenance per year, and a contingency line. Your numbers must come from your own research.
5. Why should exactly one person be “Accountable” for each deliverable in a RACI matrix?
Show the answer
If two people are accountable, each can assume the other will sign it off, and decisions stall. One accountable person gives a single point of decision.
6. Your literature review says “many ATMs exist in the world”. Improve it.
Show the answer
“ATMIA estimates about 3 million ATMs are in use worldwide, roughly one per 3,000 people [1].” — a number, a source and a citation.

Summary

Summary

  • A team of 3–5 with mixed skills, clear roles and a signed charter avoids most later conflict.
  • Choose a real, data-rich project that fits 10–12 weeks and has at least two kinds of user.
  • A proposal states the problem, SMART objectives, related work, methodology, feasibility, plan, budget and risks.
  • Feasibility (TELOS): technical, economic, legal, operational, schedule.
  • A Gantt chart shows tasks over time; watch dependencies and the critical path.
  • Risk score = probability × impact; plan to avoid, reduce, transfer or accept.
  • A RACI matrix gives every deliverable exactly one accountable person.

1 Introduction

1.1 Purpose

This report selects the software development life cycle (SDLC) model for release 1.0 of the USMS and records why. It is written after the SRS was approved (project week 5) and before design starts.

1.2 Decision in one paragraph

We will use the incremental model (26 of 30 points in the weighted matrix). The system is built in three increments of two weeks each — log in and enrollment first, then results, then fees and records. Each increment is planned as a small V: its acceptance tests are written from the SRS before coding. Two practices are borrowed from other models: a risk spike for the payment gateway in week 6 (from spiral) and a clickable prototype of the enrollment screens (from prototyping).

4.3 Checking the arithmetic

Every total was recomputed by a second team member:

  • Waterfall: 4 + 4 + 2 = 10
  • V-model: 4 + 5 + 4 + 2 = 15
  • Incremental: 4 + 6 + 5 + 5 + 4 + 2 = 26
  • Spiral: 5 + 4 + 2 = 11
  • Agile (Scrum): 6 + 5 + 4 = 15
  • Prototyping: 5 = 5

V-model and Agile tie for second place at 15. The incremental model wins by 11 points, so the decision does not depend on a close call.

4.4 The hard calls

  • Agile, C1 — No. Scrum works from a product backlog that changes every sprint; it does not build on a fixed, approved SRS.
  • Agile, C4 — No. Acceptance criteria are written per user story as the backlog evolves, not planned in advance for every SRS requirement.
  • Incremental, C5 — No. The model does not analyse risks by itself. We add that in section 6 by borrowing a risk spike from spiral.
  • Spiral, C6 — No. Each loop needs objectives, risk analysis, development and planning; three or four loops would not fit in 11 weeks with a five-person team.
  • V-model, C3 — No. The registrar office sees working software only at acceptance testing, at the end.

4.5 Would different weights change the answer?

Suppose the registrar could not review anything during the project and there were no registration deadline (C2 and C3 set to 0). Then Incremental scores 15 and the V-model 15 — a tie, which we would break in favour of the V-model for its test discipline. With our real constraints the gap is 11 points, so the choice is robust.

5 Decision and justification

“We selected the incremental model (26 of 30 points; V-model and Agile scored 15). The two criteria we weighted most — delivering enrollment before the registration window (6) and letting the registrar office review working software (5) — are exactly what incremental delivery provides: a usable log-in and enrollment system at the end of week 7, reviewed by the registrar, before results and payments are built. Because the SRS is approved, each increment takes a fixed slice of it, and we plan the acceptance tests for that slice before coding, as the V-model does. The incremental model has no risk step of its own, so we add a one-week payment-gateway spike in week 6.”

6 How we adapt the model

  1. Each increment is a small V. Review the requirements of the slice → update the design models (DFD, use cases, sequence and class diagrams) → build → unit test → integration test → acceptance demo with the registrar office using the SRS test cases (TC-nn).
  2. Risk spike (from spiral), week 6. One developer connects to the payment gateway’s sandbox and proves the request → callback → signature check (FR-19 to FR-21) before increment 3 depends on it.
  3. Clickable prototype (from prototyping), week 6. The enrollment screens are wireframed in Figma and tried by five students before they are coded (see the wireframes).
  4. Change control. The SRS stays the baseline. A new request from the registrar goes into the change log and, if accepted, into the next increment — never into the one being built.
  5. Load test every increment. From increment 1 on, a scripted test with the specified workload checks NFR-01 and NFR-04, so performance problems appear early.
